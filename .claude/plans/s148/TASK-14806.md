## Plain language — why

**What the orphan list is.** HR has two places that show employees whose month nobody can approve. One is the "mangler godkender" (missing an approver) card on each organisation's page; the other is HR's cross-organisation follow-up list, the roll-up for hand-off HRP-013. An employee on either list is an *orphan*: their month will sit unapproved because no one is there to sign it. The two must agree; the roll-up's own comment promises that they do.

**What "covered" means today, and why that is wrong for five days.** Today both reads call an employee covered whenever an open "reports to" line (a PRIMARY reporting line) points from them to a manager. Neither asks whether that manager can actually approve. Since S147 the approval routes do ask. A manager who has left stops approving the day after their last working day, although the system keeps them switched on for five more days so that *their own* manager can sign their final month (the owner's 5-day grace). Their team's months then go up the chain to the next manager. If nobody is up the chain, nobody can approve those months, yet for those five days both lists still show the team as covered by the person who left. That is a false "all clear" on the very list built to catch the problem.

**The fix.** Both reads use one definition: *an employee is covered on a day when the walk the approval routes already use names someone for that day.* The walk (`ReportingLineRepository.ResolveDesignatedApproverAsync`) already knows every rule. It skips a manager who has left, from the day after their last day. It keeps a stand-in (vikar) the manager appointed before leaving: the owner ruled in S147 that such a stand-in keeps approving until its end date (Q-B = (c)). It honours an ACTING line an administrator set. And it climbs the chain. So "covered" on HR's lists becomes exactly "someone can approve this month today". The list never decides authority itself; it asks the same question the approve button asks.

**Why one shared predicate.** If the card and the roll-up each carried their own copy of the rule, they would drift apart over time; this project has found that defect class repeatedly (PAT-030: two lists drawn from one computation agree by construction, two that merely happen to agree do not). One predicate used by both makes them equal by construction.

**The one open point, which you settle and report (R-19).** An uncovered employee who is *someone's approver* is a **root** (the top of a hierarchy, such as a director, who by design has no approver), not an orphan. Today "someone's approver" means "some person's PRIMARY line names them". Once "covered" comes from the walk, it could instead mean "the walk names them for someone", or "they hold a PRIMARY line". The readings give different answers in a few concrete shapes, listed under "Facts". You choose one, build it, and report the choice and why, with who leaves and who joins the orphan list in each shape compared with today. The paired test pass then writes the evidence fact AC-21a-d, and the Orchestrator records the wording on the HRP-013 register row (O-6). If you choose reading (ii), under which a stand-in with no approver drops off the list, that choice comes back for adjudication before the wording is recorded (ruled C2).

*Given up:* each read stops being one SQL statement, because the walk runs per employee. The approval projection has already solved that cost with a prefetched data source, and two existing performance tests bound the per-organisation read. They are this task's measurement.

## The pattern

**Generate the domain, left-join what exists, and give absence an explicit name.**

1. **Generate** the domain the report is about — here, the months each employment window covers, via `generate_series`
   from a ruled floor to the current month.
2. **`LEFT JOIN`** the rows that exist, on an exact key (S140 joined `period_type = 'MONTHLY' AND period_start = …
   AND period_end = …`, served by the `UNIQUE (employee_id, period_start, period_end)` index, so a weekly period
   inside the month can never be mistaken for the month's row).
3. **`COALESCE` the missing state to an explicit sentinel** — `COALESCE(status, 'NONE')` — so no predicate silently
   evaporates to NULL. A bare `status <> 'APPROVED'` evaluates to NULL for a missing row and **drops the very case
   the report exists to surface**.
4. **Serve every classification from that one statement** with parameter-driven predicates.

Two properties fall out for free, and they are the reason to do it this way:

- **Sibling lists drawn over the SAME population partition by construction, not by agreement.** Two lists built from one
  enumeration with complementary status predicates over the same rows — S140's "employee late" and "approver late" — cannot
  both contain the same row, and nothing can fall between them. Two separately written predicates that *happen* to agree
  are exactly the class of bug this project keeps finding. **Read the scope of that claim carefully:** it holds only while
  the population is shared. S140 also has two lists that are **disjoint but not complementary** (one requires APPROVED, the
  other requires not-APPROVED, so no month is on both) whose *populations differ* — one is restricted to each leaver's
  final month by owner ruling, the other spans everyone — and the sprint had to **retract** a contract that claimed they
  partitioned. See the guard-rail below; do not carry the stronger reading away from this paragraph.
- **A summary count cannot disagree with its own list.** Implement `?summary=true` by omitting the list from the
  **wire**, not by writing a second `COUNT(*)` query — a second copy of the predicate is a second thing to keep in
  sync. Count ≡ list length, always.

## Cost characteristic

The enumeration is O(employees in scope × months in the floor) rows before filtering — roughly 26,000 for a
2,000-person organisation over 13 months. Bounded by the floor, filtered in SQL so few rows return, joined on a unique
index. "Correct first, fast later" was the deliberate call; if a landing page ever feels slow, that belongs in the
performance register rather than in a premature aggregate.
