## Plain language — why

**What TASK-14831 changes.** Payroll records a month (the export) and corrects one (`/recalculate`) only in its turn on the system-wide "pay inputs" lock, the same lock every change dated into the past takes. Inside its turn it derives the month's inputs again and refuses "calculate again" (409 `payroll-inputs-changed`) if anything it calculated with has changed, writing nothing.

**What you prove.**
1. **Either order is safe** (AC-PIL-a … d, g). When the change wins the race, payroll refuses and the retry pays the new values; when payroll wins, the change finds the recorded month and puts it on HR's worklist. You force each order deterministically: you hold the lock yourself, start the two requests one after the other, wait until PostgreSQL shows each waiting on the lock, then let go. PostgreSQL grants a waiting lock in queue order, so the test, not timing, decides who goes first.
2. **The check is complete** (AC-PIL-f): it notices one changed thing at a time, a change dated inside the month that splits it, and a day it cannot account for, and lets equal inputs through.
3. **Who takes the lock, first and alone** (AC-PIL-e row (3)): the export and the correction now wait on it holding nothing else; with rows (1) and (2) already in the file, every taker in the code has a fact.
4. **What the lock costs** (three figures, recorded, not judged): how long a large publish keeps recordings waiting; how long a recording waits when an HR drawer save queues behind a Skema save; and what a burst of profile corrections does to a small database connection pool (SEC-066).

Expected values come from the plan and the briefs, never from what the code returns.
