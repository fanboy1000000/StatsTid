## Plain language — why

TASK-14821 closes a race: a correction dated back into a month and a payroll recording of that month could each miss the other, leaving the month paid on stale values with nobody told. It does five things you prove (the fifth added in draft 3).

1. **Seven Backend writers take one shared "pay inputs" lock as their very first step, and hold nothing else while they wait for it.** "Nothing else" is the point. A writer that held some other lock while waiting could form a circle with another request, and the whole system would stall. So each fact holds the lock itself, starts the request, waits until PostgreSQL shows the request waiting on that one lock, and then checks, from PostgreSQL's own lock table, that the waiting request holds nothing except its own transaction id. Two negatives prove the lock is not taken where it should not be.
2. **The employment start date, the end date and a settlement reversal that corrects the end date now tell HR.** When such a change reaches a month already sent to payroll, or a settled holiday year, it lands on HR's worklist under the reason `EMPLOYMENT_WINDOW_CHANGE`, dated from the earliest day the change can affect, and pointing at the event that caused it. An end date saved again unchanged lists nothing.
3. **Nobody changes their own pay inputs.** An HR user who changes their own agreement code, hours fraction, position or category is refused with 403, and nothing is written; so is an HR user who closes their own profile (added at the adjudication). Saving their own e-mail through the edit drawer, which re-sends every field unchanged, still works. A departed HR user gets the departed refusal first.
4. **Closing a profile tells HR** when payroll has already recorded the current month: that month lands on the worklist under the profile-change reason. One fact proves it now, in the wave that builds it, rather than waiting for wave 5's race tests.
5. **A change never lands on someone who moved institution mid-request** (SEC-062; draft 3). Six writers check the administrator's scope against the person's organisation before locking the person's row. If a transfer commits in between, the write would land on a person the administrator was never checked for. Each writer now compares, under its lock, the organisation its check approved with the one it locked, and refuses with 409 "refresh and retry". You prove each of the six by forcing exactly that interleaving: the test holds the person's row, starts the request, waits until PostgreSQL shows it waiting, moves the person to another institution, and only then lets the request continue. TASK-14820-T proved the user edit the same way (`TransferRaceTests`).

**How the concurrency facts stay deterministic.** Nothing here relies on timing. The test, not a race, decides the order: it holds the lock first, and PostgreSQL grants a waiting lock in queue order. "Observed waiting" is a bounded wait on a condition that PostgreSQL reports (`pg_locks`), never a sleep. A negative fact proves "does not take the lock" by completing *while the test still holds it*, which a request that takes the lock cannot do.

**Worked example (AC-PIL-h (8)).** Employee B has no recorded start date, June 2026 is already sent to payroll, and her registrations begin on the 15th. HR sets her start to Monday 15 June 2026. Before: nothing on the worklist. After: June is listed with the reason "employment-window change", dated Monday 1 June 2026. June opens on a Monday, and that is the earliest day an unknown start can have affected.

Expected values come from the plan and this brief, never from what the code returns.
